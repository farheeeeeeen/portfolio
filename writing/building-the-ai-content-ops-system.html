Building EquityList's AI Content Operations System

I'd never written a script in my life. Over one week, working with Claude Code as a personal side project, I found out how much of my own job could be turned into a system — and whether the team could eventually run the content engine without me.

I write for founders, CFOs, and HR/People Ops at Indian startups, Seed through Series C, expanding into the US. EquityList is an equity-management platform — cap tables, ESOPs, fundraising, compliance. Nobody asked me to build this system. I started it as a side project, out of curiosity about a specific question: how much of my actual day-to-day could I automate, and could I get it far enough that the team could eventually run the content engine without needing me in the loop.

The day-to-day I wanted to automate looked like this. To plan a quarter, I'd go through our existing blog list by hand to see what we'd already covered and where the gaps were, pull ideas out of that gap, look up each candidate keyword in Semrush one at a time to check the volume, tag every surviving topic as TOFU, MOFU, or BOFU, and then prioritize the list by feel. Separately, once posts were live, I'd pull performance data out of Search Console and HubSpot and manually reconcile the two into something that actually told me whether a post was working. Neither step was guesswork — I had real data in Semrush and Search Console the whole time. The work was doing all of that by hand, one keyword and one export at a time, with no link between "here's the data" and "here's what to do about it."

Week one, and I'd never opened Claude Code before

This was the first time I'd used Claude Code at all, not just the first time I'd built anything with it. I'd never written a script before, never touched Python or JSON, not even to read one. Claude wrote every line of code in the system. My job was to describe what I needed in plain English, test what came back against data I already trusted, and push on the parts that didn't work yet.

I didn't move on to the next piece until the one in front of me actually worked. That's the order the system grew in, and it's the same order the manual pain showed up in.

First: the quarterly calendar

The calendar was the most painful part, so it's what I built first. This became the /content-strategy skill: gap analysis against the existing blog inventory, keyword discovery, funnel-stage tagging, and prioritization, all in one pass instead of four separate manual steps.

Getting the shortlisting right took a real failure first. The first time I actually ran it, the topics it handed back didn't hold up — some were technically wrong terms with high volume, some were things we'd already written and ruled out, some just weren't things a founder or CFO would ever search. That run is what told me a keyword list alone wasn't enough; it needed guardrails in front of it that would catch a bad topic before it ever reached the calendar. The seven quality filters below came out of that one bad run, not from planning ahead of time:

Quality filters — topic shortlisting
  • Audience match — would a founder or CFO actually search this, not an employee or a retail trader?
  • Domain relevance — is it actually about equity management, not generic startup advice?
  • Intent specificity — does the volume come from the right audience? "Capital gain tax on sale of shares" is mostly retail traders; the real target is "capital gains on unlisted shares."
  • Terminology accuracy — reject a topic built around a technically wrong term, even if that term has more search volume.
  • No duplication — check the existing inventory and keyword status before ever suggesting a topic twice.
  • Lead-magnet intent — a keyword implying "calculator" or "template" doesn't belong in the content calendar at all.
  • Factual currency — reject topics built on laws that have since changed.

Writing those down fixed more than that one run. The "no duplication" filter depends on every keyword carrying a status — active, planned, rejected, researched — and building that out surfaced two more bugs in the same pass: the keyword tracker wasn't actually preserving that status between runs, so a topic I'd already rejected could quietly come back a week later, and the calendar's file paths were hardcoded to a single quarter, so the whole thing would have broken the moment Q3 planning started. Both were caught early, while I was still checking every output closely, and both got fixed before I trusted the calendar enough to move on.

Then: performance tracking

Once the calendar was functional, the next manual bottleneck was the one on the other end — pulling Search Console and HubSpot data separately and reconciling them by hand to see if any of it was actually working. This became /analyze-performance: one report that merges SEO performance with lead attribution, so I can see quick wins, underperforming posts, and internal-linking gaps in one place instead of two spreadsheets.

This is where the most consequential bug showed up. An early funnel-analysis run reported fifteen deals stuck at the MQL stage. I happened to know the real number from HubSpot directly, and it was twenty-eight — not a rounding difference, a real miscount in the funnel logic. I caught it by reading the report against what I already knew, not by reviewing the code, and that's the pattern that mattered: building fast with AI doesn't remove the need for someone checking the math, it just moves where that checking happens, from writing the calculation yourself to refusing to trust the output until you've verified it against something real. Later, once the system had been running long enough that I was checking things less closely, the month-on-month and quarter-on-quarter calculations turned out to be wrong too — caught the same way, because someone was actually using the numbers for a real decision and the numbers didn't hold up.

What came out of that week

By the end of the week, the system had four layers. Skills sit on top — the natural-language commands I actually type. Below that, a sub-agent handles competitor research so it doesn't clutter the main planning conversation. Below that, 18 Python scripts do the orchestration: hitting APIs, processing data, syncing to Sheets. At the bottom, a data layer — JSON for anything nested and stateful, CSV for anything flat and meant to be opened in a spreadsheet. All of it reads from HubSpot, Google Search Console, Plausible, Semrush, and Webflow, and writes out to Google Sheets.

Five skills in total, the two above plus three I added once those were solid:

  • /content-strategy — the quarterly planning engine described above: gap analysis, competitor research, keyword discovery, the seven quality filters, volume validation, prioritization, a ranked calendar.
  • /analyze-performance — the weekly SEO-plus-lead-attribution report described above.
  • /categorize-blogs — turns a raw list of titles and URLs into a structured inventory: category, funnel stage, geography, content type, status.
  • /new-blog-published — the post-publish checklist, automated: add to inventory, register the keyword, flip the calendar status from Planned to Published, suggest internal links.
  • /lead-magnets — scans for keywords that imply a tool or template rather than a blog post, scores the opportunity, and outputs a build brief.

One thing I deliberately kept outside this system: actually drafting and reviewing the blog posts themselves. I already had a separate Claude Projects setup for that, built a while before this project — Claude Code isn't the strongest tool for long-form writing, so I left that work exactly where it was and built this system for everything around the writing instead.

The parts that run without me

Two automations went live that same week and still run without me touching anything. Monday at 9am, a HubSpot inbound-lead journey report lands in a new Google Sheets tab, tracking every inbound contact since the previous Monday. Monday at 10am, the inbound funnel analysis follows: stage breakdown, conversion rates against targets, stalled-deal alerts, written specifically so a non-technical marketing lead can read it unassisted and know what to do next.

One thing is deliberately not automated: syncing the content calendar to Google Sheets requires an explicit flag. The sheet has manual formatting layered on top of it — red for rejected, strikethrough for removed — and a blind auto-sync would wipe that out. Leaving that one step manual, on purpose, was the harder call to make than automating it would have been.

A couple of smaller decisions came from the same instinct: build for what the moment actually needs, even when full automation would have been easier to build. The blog inventory alone is over 100KB of JSON, so instead of loading the whole file into a conversation, a query script answers "do we already have this?" directly — it's a hard rule in the project's own instructions now, never read the full inventory file. And HubSpot data gets pulled into weekly snapshots instead of queried live, because a live pull every time risks rate limits and numbers that don't match from one week to the next.

What actually carried forward

The same method got reused recently for a different product line — scoping content strategy for EquityList's secretarial-compliance product, a different surface, same company. It formalized into a repeatable eight-step narrowing cycle: map the full surface area before touching keywords, stress-test the lifecycle, validate volumes, apply inclusion filters, classify every rejected topic with a reason, reconcile keyword-gated against moment-gated views, tag, and route anything that isn't content to an off-blog asset instead.

That's the part I'd point to if someone asked what this project actually produced. Not the eighteen scripts or the five skills — those are one implementation, and implementations change. What carried over to a second product was the method underneath them: a repeatable way of turning judgment calls I used to make one at a time, from memory, into something written down, checked, and reusable.
